I write software for a living. Then I close the work laptop, open my own, and keep going.

That sounds like a lot. It isn't, if you're honest about how little time you actually have. Right now that time goes into [Castfy](https://castfy.app), which I'm building in public. A few focused hours a week is the real budget. Everything else follows from that.

## Scope is the whole game

With a day job, I can't out-work anyone. So I out-cut them.

Every feature has to answer one question: does this get the product in front of someone sooner? If not, it waits. Settings pages wait. Admin dashboards wait. "It would be nice if" waits forever.

## Small sessions, finished things

I try to end every session with something done. Not "made progress on". Done. A fixed bug, a merged branch, a screen that works.

An hour that ends with something shipped beats three hours that end with a half-finished refactor I have to remember tomorrow.

## Pick boring tools

The day job already gives me enough new problems. On the side, I use what I know: Next.js, React, Node, Postgres. Boring tools mean my limited time goes into the product, not into learning the stack.

## Ship before it feels ready

I post demos before things are polished and ask people straight up: is this ready for beta? The answers are more useful than another week of polishing alone.

## Solo is a feature

No meetings. No waiting for approval. I decide in the morning and ship at night.

The cost is that nobody catches my mistakes. So I keep things simple enough that I can catch them myself.

That's the whole system. Small scope, finished sessions, boring tools. It's slow, but it compounds.
